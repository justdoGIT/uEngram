# Streaming anomaly detection: calibration, robustness, evaluation

## 1. Metrics and time-series evaluation

At 2–3% prevalence, an always-normal classifier scores **97–98% accuracy** while finding zero anomalies. Accuracy therefore obscures the rare-event operating trade-off. Report threshold-free **AUROC** (ranking: $P(s^+>s^-)$), but emphasize **AUPRC / average precision** because its precision baseline equals prevalence and it exposes false positives in rare-event settings. Also report (i) event-wise recall at a predeclared false-alarm budget (e.g. false alarms per machine-day or per 1,000 normal windows), and (ii) event detection delay, $d_i=t_{alarm,i}-t_{onset,i}$, with missed events separately counted/censored. Thresholds must be selected on calibration/validation streams, not test labels. These are recommended reporting choices, not claims of a universal metric standard.

Time-series scoring needs event semantics. **Point adjustment (PA)** retroactively marks every point of a labeled anomaly interval as detected if even one point in it was flagged; Kim et al. demonstrate that this can inflate scores—even random scores can appear state-of-the-art—and distort rankings. Do not use PA as the headline result. [Kim et al., AAAI-22](https://aaai.org/papers/07194-towards-a-rigorous-evaluation-of-time-series-anomaly-detection/)

Tatbul et al. generalize precision/recall to intervals, making overlap amount, positional bias (e.g. reward early detection), and fragmentation/cardinality explicit. Their range recall averages per-real-event recall; overlap reward may be position-weighted, and cardinality can discount multiple predicted fragments. Choose these settings from maintenance consequences, publish them, and retain pointwise metrics for comparability. [Tatbul et al., NeurIPS 2018](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/file/8f468c873a32bb0619eaeb2050ba45d1-Paper.pdf)

NAB is a complementary streaming score: it rewards early detections within labeled windows, penalizes false alarms, and offers application profiles. It is not a substitute for interpretable event precision/recall or a universal score; Tatbul et al. note its fixed early-detection bias and scoring limitations. [Lavin & Ahmad, NAB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.03336); [Tatbul et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/file/8f468c873a32bb0619eaeb2050ba45d1-Paper.pdf). Benchmark conclusions also require caution: Wu & Keogh argue that popular TSAD exemplars often have flaws that make comparisons unreliable and apparent progress illusory. Use time-ordered splits, event labels and transparent protocols; test across machines/regimes and synthetic controlled cases, rather than tuning to one benchmark. [Wu & Keogh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13807)

## 2. Calibrating thresholds with conformal scores

A slot's $D^2=(z-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(z-\mu)$ has a $\chi^2_{16}$ reference only under a correctly specified Gaussian model with known/appropriate covariance and in-distribution samples. Real slot distributions, whitening error, small counts, diagonal covariance, and regime mixtures violate that model; its nominal tail probability need not equal the live false-alarm rate.

Instead define a nonconformity score $a(x)$ (for example, minimum slot Mahalanobis distance, possibly computed against a frozen normal reference). On a held-out **normal** calibration set of size $m$, store scores $a_1,…,a_m$. For new score $a_*$, the inductive conformal upper-tail p-value is

$$p(x_*)=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf1[a_i\ge a_*]}{m+1};\qquad alarm\iff p(x_*)\le\alpha.$$

Under exchangeability of calibration and test normals, this controls the **marginal** false-positive probability at at most $\alpha$ (ties handled conservatively); resolution is $1/(m+1)$. [Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer, *Algorithmic Learning in a Random World*](https://www.springer.com/gp/book/9780387001524); [Bates et al., *Testing for Outliers with Conformal p-values*, Annals of Statistics 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08279). Laxhammar & Falkman apply inductive conformal anomaly detection to sequential trajectories/sub-trajectories. [Paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10472-013-9381-7).

For this engine, calibrate per operating context (or use a context-conditional/conservative pooled score), then alarm on calibrated tail p-values rather than a fixed $\chi^2$ quantile. To preserve validity, exclude the candidate event from the reference, use a calibration buffer not continually contaminated by alarms, and treat drift as invalidating exchangeability: recalibrate only after a confirmed stable-normal segment. **Do not promise per-window or indefinite-stream FAR control from ordinary conformal validity**; it is marginal under exchangeability, and serially dependent, drifting sensor streams need block/online methods or empirical FAR monitoring. Bates et al. study stronger outlier-testing guarantees, but their assumptions/design should be selected explicitly.

## 3. Masking: anomalies learned as normal

A nearest-neighbor score commonly takes the distance to the $k$th nearest reference observation (or a local-density variant); adding a recurring anomaly to that reference creates close neighbors, reducing its novelty score. Likewise, absorbing anomalies into broad Gaussian slots shifts the mean/inflates variance and makes future anomalous points less surprising—**masking**. Conversely, contaminated points can make ordinary observations look unusual (**swamping**). Classical outlier work documents both phenomena; one should not equate a low score after online adaptation with evidence that the behavior is safe. [Hadi, *Identifying Multiple Outliers in Multivariate Data*, JRSS B (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1992.tb01892.x); [Rousseeuw & Van Zomeren, robust distance/outlier detection](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1990.10474920).

Keep alarmed/unconfirmed observations out of normal-state updates; maintain a separate episodic/anomaly buffer, and require persistence/independent evidence before promotion into normal memory. Compare against static-reference and contamination-resistant baselines. Goldstein & Uchida compare unsupervised methods, including nearest-neighbor approaches; the lesson is score/dataset dependence, not one universally best detector. [Goldstein & Uchida, *A Comparative Evaluation of Unsupervised Anomaly Detection Algorithms*, PLOS ONE 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0152173).

## 4. Streaming micro-clusters and slot policy

**CluStream** (Aggarwal et al., 2003) summarizes clusters with micro-cluster sufficient statistics (count/linear and squared sums), maintains them online, then answers clustering queries from stored snapshots at different time horizons. It uses a fixed micro-cluster budget and geometric snapshots; this is compact but can force merging/pruning when the number of states changes. [Original paper, VLDB 2003](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2003/papers/S04P02.pdf).

**DenStream** is a closer model for “new state versus noise.” It maintains potential micro-clusters (established dense states) and outlier micro-clusters (candidate seeds) in a separate buffer. A point first tries nearest potential cluster, accepted only if the resulting radius stays within $\epsilon$; otherwise it tries the outlier buffer, promotes a candidate when its faded weight exceeds $\beta\mu$, or starts a new outlier candidate. It fades weights as $f(\Delta t)=2^{-\lambda\Delta t}$ and prunes stale/low-weight candidates. Thus repeated novelty accumulates evidence without immediately declaring every singleton a new normal cluster. [Cao et al., DenStream, SDM 2006](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~ester/papers/SDM2006.DenStream.final.pdf).

**D-Stream** is a grid-based density stream clustering algorithm; **MCOD** (micro-cluster-based outlier detection) maintains micro-clusters and detects outliers based on neighbor counts within a sliding window. They offer relevant alternatives when density/local-neighbor semantics fit better than Gaussian ellipsoids. [D-Stream, Chen & Tu 2007](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDE.2007.367); [MCOD, Kontaki et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDE.2011.5767922). Practical takeaway: represent state evidence with bounded local spread plus support/age, use a candidate buffer and persistence criterion, and make outliers unable to enlarge mature normal states. No paper guarantees these methods outperform a fitted state model on these specific machine streams.

## 5. Drift, recurrence, and supersession

Drift detection should monitor more than raw feature means: monitor calibrated normal-score/false-alarm statistics and state-conditioned residuals. **ADWIN** maintains an adaptive window and tests subwindow means; when a sufficiently large difference is found it drops stale history. Its paper gives a cut threshold depending on confidence $\delta$, subwindow sizes and observed means, and guarantees bounded false-positive probability under its assumptions. [Bifet & Gavaldà, 2007](https://www.cs.upc.edu/~gavalda/papers/adwin06.pdf). **DDM** monitors a predictor's online error rate and its standard deviation, signaling warning/drift when error rises relative to its historical minimum; it is supervised/error-feedback oriented, so for unlabeled AD, feed it delayed adjudication or proxy signals, not unverified anomaly alarms. [Gama et al., original drift detection method](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-45728-7_20).

A drift alarm says “distribution changed,” not “old state is obsolete.” To handle recurring concepts, retain a bounded library of frozen state prototypes with last-seen time, support and score calibration; on change, test new data against both current and archived prototypes, reactivate a close archived state, or create a candidate. Mark the former current state superseded only after persistent evidence that the new state is stable and distinct. Recurrent-concept / model-reuse research motivates this memory-pool approach, but exact retention policy and capacity require validation for this sensor task. [Gama et al., *Learning with Drift Detection*, Brazilian Symposium on AI 2004](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-28645-5_29); [Losing et al., recurring concept drift / model reuse](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02804).

## 6. Persistence, hysteresis and transient alarms

Industrial alarm management distinguishes an alarm condition from transient threshold crossings. A **on-delay** requires a condition to persist for $T_{on}$ before annunciation; **off-delay/deadband** delays clearing or requires the signal to return a margin inside the boundary. Hysteresis can be written: enter alarm when $s>\theta_{on}$, clear only when $s<\theta_{off}$, with $\theta_{off}<\theta_{on}$. Persistence converts brief excursions (such as operating-transition ramps) into no alarm, at the cost of at least the chosen delay on sustained events and potentially missing short faults. Choose $T_{on}$ below the shortest actionable fault duration and explicitly report the resulting delay/missed-event trade-off; no source establishes a universal best timer for this stream.

ISA-18.2 alarm-management guidance names deadbands and delay times as basic alarm attributes and says deadbands can be effective for process-variable measurement noise; it treats configuration as application-specific. EEMUA 191 is a complementary alarm-system design/management guide. No defensible universal numeric timer/deadband range was verified here—set values from sensor noise, physical transition duration, hazard response time and operator study, then validate event recall and false alarms. [ANSI/ISA-18.2-2009](https://ia800809.us.archive.org/12/items/RP120601/18.2.pdf); [ISA-TR18.2.5-2022 preview](https://www.isa.org/getmedia/3b68ebbf-eff7-476a-ad57-7b60072c7e8a/TR_18-2-5_Preview.pdf); [EEMUA Publication 191](https://www.eemua.org/products/publications/digital/eemua-publication-191).
